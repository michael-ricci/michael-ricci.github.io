Crime in Syracuse:
What the Data Actually Shows

People in Syracuse talk about crime constantly, but the conversation is rarely grounded in data. Decades of reported crime figures tell a more complicated story than most residents realize.

By Michael Ricci Published April 2026 Syracuse University · Newhouse School

Ask almost anyone in Syracuse about crime and they'll tell you it's getting worse. It's a feeling that shapes where people choose to live, how politicians campaign and how the city spends money on policing. But the data doesn't always back that up. A look at decades of reported crime figures from the Syracuse Police Department and surrounding Onondaga County agencies shows a more complicated picture.

Crime in Syracuse is down significantly from where it was in the early 1990s. But after hitting a record low in 2021, it has climbed back up sharply. And comparing Syracuse to its suburban neighbors raises questions that go beyond just counting incidents.

A City's Crime Trajectory

Total reported crime in Syracuse peaked at 12,706 incidents in 1991 and has fallen by nearly 43 percent since then, reaching 7,259 in 2024. Through most of the 2000s and 2010s the decline was fairly steady, following a pattern seen in a lot of mid-sized American cities as policing strategies shifted and economic conditions changed in certain neighborhoods.

The steepest drop came between 2013 and 2021, when total crime fell from 7,665 to an all-time low of 4,736. Since then it has gone back up every single year, rising more than 53 percent in three years to reach 7,259 in 2024, nearly wiping out a decade of progress. Onondaga County Executive Ryan McMahon acknowledged that poverty is a persistent problem in Syracuse and that dealing with it is central to any serious effort to improve public safety in the city.

That matters when reading this data. Syracuse has one of the highest poverty rates of any city its size in the country, and poverty is one of the most reliable predictors of crime. The long-term decline didn't happen because poverty went away. It remains a serious problem, and the recent rise in crime has renewed questions about what is actually driving it.

Violent Crime vs. Property Crime: Two Different Stories

Not all crime categories are moving the same way. Violent crime, which includes murder, rape, robbery and aggravated assault, peaked in 2005 at 1,570 incidents. It has never really come back down to the levels seen in the 1990s. In 2024, Syracuse recorded 1,111 violent crimes, and the number has stayed above 1,000 for most of the past two decades.

Property crime is a different story, and it's mostly responsible for the recent surge. After dropping to a record low of 3,508 incidents in 2021, property crime climbed to 6,148 by 2024. The biggest driver of that jump is motor vehicle theft, which went from 506 incidents in 2018 to 1,270 in 2024, a 151 percent increase in six years. The spike is partly tied to a national trend involving security flaws in certain Hyundai and Kia models that made them easy to steal. In Syracuse, where property crime was already a problem, the increase has been hard to ignore.

How Does Syracuse Compare to Its Suburbs?

The difference between Syracuse and its suburbs is hard to overstate. In 2024, North Syracuse, a village of roughly 8,000 people a few miles north of the city, reported just 100 total crimes. Syracuse, with a population of around 148,000, reported 7,259. The population difference is about 18 to 1. The crime gap is much larger than that.

North Syracuse has stayed relatively flat across the entire 35-year span of this data, ranging from about 58 to 309 incidents per year with no major trend up or down. It hasn't gone through anything close to what Syracuse has experienced. According to McMahon, the concentration of poverty inside Syracuse's city limits is a big part of what separates the city's public safety situation from the suburbs around it, which generally have higher incomes and less economic stress.

What the Data Can and Can't Tell Us

There are real limits to what this data can show. All of the figures here are reported crimes, meaning someone called it in and law enforcement recorded it. A lot of crime never gets reported, especially certain kinds of assault and theft. The actual volume of crime in Syracuse is probably higher than what shows up in these numbers.

Comparing data across 35 years also has complications. How crimes are classified and reported has changed over time, so some of the variation from year to year may reflect shifts in recordkeeping rather than actual changes in what happened. And because the dataset doesn't include population figures, it isn't possible to calculate per-capita rates without pulling in additional data. That caveat matters especially when comparing Syracuse to a small village like North Syracuse.

About the Data & Methodology

This analysis uses reported crime data from the Onondaga County crime dataset, which includes figures from the Syracuse Police Department, North Syracuse Village PD, and East Syracuse Village PD. Data spans from 1990 to 2024. Analysis was conducted in Google Sheets. All figures represent reported crimes as submitted to Onondaga County. Live source: Ryan McMahon, Onondaga County Executive.
